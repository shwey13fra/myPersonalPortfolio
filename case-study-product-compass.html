Context

I built Product Compass while doing the exact thing it's designed to help with: positioning myself for product roles. That overlap wasn't incidental — it was the point. I wanted to study the PM job search the way a PM studies any problem: talk to the people living it, map what already exists, and only then decide what's worth building. The result is a live product at product-compass-two.vercel.app — built discovery-first from a research PRD, not a feature list.

The people I was designing for were PMs and aspiring PMs actively job-searching — a group I could reach honestly because I was one of them. I deliberately recruited across very different backgrounds so that any shared pain would mean more, not less. The aim from the start was behaviour creation, not feature creation: help people discover the right opportunities, position themselves for each one, and borrow credibility from a network — with every feature earning its place against that outcome.

Problem

The instinct in a job search is to blame capability: I must not be good enough. The research pointed somewhere less comfortable and more useful. Candidates mostly don't fail because they lack ability — they fail because the signal around them is broken. They can't tell which roles are real, they can't adapt their story to each role's context, and after applying they hear nothing at all.

PM hiring is unusually contextual. The same person is a different candidate for an AI role, a fintech role, a B2B-platform role, and a 0-to-1 consumer role — different lead story, different metrics, different framing. Yet almost everyone applies with one generic resume to everything, and reuses it everywhere. The core thesis that anchored the whole product: candidates fail on positioning, signal, and feedback — not on capability. That reframe is what decides which features are worth building and which are noise.

Research Approach

I ran discovery before committing to any feature. Three in-depth qualitative interviews with PMs actively job-searching, chosen as deliberately different archetypes: an HR-tech SaaS PM with about a year in the title, an unemployed enterprise-SaaS PM optimising for speed of re-entry, and a BFSI-and-process professional targeting a first consumer-product role in a scarce local market. Three people who agree on a pain despite having almost nothing else in common is a far stronger signal than three similar people agreeing.

I clustered what they told me into a pain register — nine themes from the interviews, each scored on two axes: frequency (how many of the three raised it) and intensity (merely mentioned, versus named as a top time-sink or a "solve-this-overnight" wish). Then I ran a secondary teardown across public review aggregators and product sites for eight named competitors, and merged both sources into a single twelve-point register. I was careful about what review data can and can't say: it self-selects for the vocal and frustrated, so it tells you which pains are salient, not how common they are.

The synthesis was sharp. The deepest, most universal pain was the silence after applying — every interviewee felt qualified and heard nothing back. The funnel broke at both ends, not the middle: time bled at discovery (which companies, are they real, are they still open?) and at tailoring (story and resume, per role), while writing the application itself was comparatively easy — exactly the two ends worth targeting. And every interviewee had already DIY-ed half of this, badly: tracker sheets, a manual Notion board, a hand-built AI "skill", even a paid subscription bought specifically to stop getting stuck. People hacking together bad versions and paying for them is about as strong a demand signal as discovery produces.

Key Decisions & Tradeoffs

Decision 01

Compete on positioning, not on another filtered job feed

Why: The obvious build is a better search experience, and I explicitly ruled it out. Discovery UX is already owned by a strong de-noised aggregator; salary-transparent tech matching is owned by two others. Entering as "another filtered search engine" is a losing game. The one thing no competitor did — across all eight I tore down — was adapt the candidate's story to a role's context. That white space maps directly to the thesis, so it became the wedge: a positioning brief that picks the lead story, re-angles the metrics, and produces a short pitch for one specific role. The tradeoff is that positioning is harder to build and harder to explain than search — but it's the only defensible ground.

Decision 02

A lightweight feedback signal, not a full application tracker

Why: The deepest emotional pain was the black box after applying, and the obvious response is a tracker. All three interviewees already ran manual trackers — real, repeated pain. But building a full tracker pulls the product straight toward becoming an ATS: a crowded space with high build cost and little differentiation. So I drew the line at a light "seen / shortlisted / closed" status that rides on ecosystem hiring, plus a follow-up-or-move-on nudge. It addresses the emotional core without committing to a category I'd lose in. Tracking stays a support feature, never the product.

Decision 03

Curate verified, genuinely-PM roles — resist the odds score and cold-email automation

Why: The highest-intensity single wish was unglamorous: "show me roles that are real, live, and actually product." That's curation, and it's exactly where communities prove demand but burn out — one paused its jobs board, others run on volunteers, open channels fill with scams. Doing curation and trust systematically is the moat. I deliberately resisted two tempting adjacent features: a probability / odds score (ATS-like, expensive, dubious accuracy) and generic cold-email automation (drifts into LinkedIn-competitor territory). Both were real requests. Both were out of scope. Saying no to real requests is the decision I'm most confident about.

What Shipped

Shipped wedge — not a validated business

The live product ships the wedge, not the whole vision. The research tells me the positioning gap is real and unowned; it does not yet tell me this particular product closes it at scale. I'm drawing that line on purpose — being honest about where a product actually stands is part of good PM thinking.

Live MVP

These describe what's built and live today — not measured outcomes from sustained usage.

Three things work today. A positioning tool that turns your experience into a tailored brief for one specific role — a lead story, re-angled metrics, and a 60-second pitch. A role-authenticity score from 0 to 100 that separates true product ownership from delivery-only coordination wearing the PM title. And a referral pathway that lets a candidate talk to an internal advocate privately, with status visible but message content never exposed to an admin. Browsing curated roles needs no sign-up; the account and referral layer sit behind it.

I want to be precise about stage. This is a shipped wedge grounded in strong discovery — not a proven product-market fit. The two open questions the research can't answer for me are the supply side (a reliable stream of verified, genuinely-PM roles is operationally hard — exactly as it was for the communities I studied) and willingness to pay for positioning rather than just discovery. Both need cohorts of real usage, not more analysis.

Reflections

The most valuable thing discovery gave me was a reason to build less. My instinct was to solve the whole funnel — discovery, tailoring, tracking, outreach, feedback. The pain register showed the funnel breaks at two ends; the competitive teardown showed only one of those ends was unowned. That's how a sprawling idea became a single wedge. The discipline wasn't in what I added — it was in what I refused to add despite real users asking for it.

The trap I was most wary of was building "another filtered search engine." It's the easiest thing to build, the easiest to demo, and the fastest way to lose, because the incumbents already own it. Staying on the harder, narrower ground of positioning was uncomfortable precisely because it's harder to show in a screenshot — but comfort isn't a product strategy.

What I'd stress-test next isn't the product, it's the supply side and willingness to pay. I've watched community job boards prove this exact demand and then buckle under the operational cost of curation and trust. Whether I can do that systematically, cohort after cohort, is the open question I won't have an answer to until it either holds or doesn't. That's the thing I'd validate before building anything more.
